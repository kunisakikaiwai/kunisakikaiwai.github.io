Every visitor to Japan learns the rule on day one. **Shrines are Shinto**: torii gates, clap twice, home of the kami. **Temples are Buddhist**: incense, statues, bow to the Buddha. Two religions, two architectures, two etiquettes — tidy.

Here is the thing the guidebooks rarely add: **for most of Japanese history, that tidy division did not exist.** For over a thousand years, gods and buddhas shared the same sacred grounds, the same festivals, and sometimes the same body. The Japanese term for this — *shinbutsu-shūgō*, "the fusion of kami and buddhas" — came up constantly in [the Kunisaki Chronicle](/en/story/).

Which raises today's question. If gods and buddhas were simply *together*, all along… **who needed a special word for it, and when?**

### The word is younger than it looks

Start with a confession: *shinbutsu-shūgō* is not an ancient term. No one in Nara, Kamakura, or Edo-period Japan used it. It is a **modern label, coined by scholars looking backward**.

And of course it is — because you can only call something "mixed" if you know what "separate" looks like. For the farmer praying to the rice-field god in the morning and asking a Buddhist priest to pray over a sick child in the evening, this was not fusion. It was just life. Nobody needs a special word for eating fish with rice.

So before we find out who needed the word, let's look at what was actually happening — because "the two were mixed" turns out to be far too crude a summary.

### The merging had stages — and the relationship kept flipping

**Stage one: buddhas rescue the gods.** The earliest idea, strange and rather moving: the kami themselves are unenlightened beings, suffering like us, and they *want Buddhist salvation*. So temples were built for the gods' own sake — "shrine-temples" (jingūji). At Usa, this was Miroku-ji ([tale 6](/en/story/06-shinbutsu/)). In this arrangement, the buddhas are the rescuers, the gods the rescued.

**Stage two: gods protect the Dharma.** Next, the kami become allies — guardians of Buddhism. The banner case was Hachiman backing the casting of Nara's Great Buddha and traveling to the capital to venerate it in person (749). Now the two are partners with a common cause.

**Stage three: gods enter the path.** In 781 the court granted Hachiman the title **Great Bodhisattva** — a god formally walking the Buddhist path, later portrayed in sculpture as a **monk with shaved head and robes**. The god has become, in effect, the Buddha's disciple.

**Stage four: gods and buddhas merge.** Finally, from the late Heian period, the elegant doctrine called *honji suijaku*: the kami are **local manifestations of the buddhas** — the same beings, wearing familiar Japanese faces to reach the people. At Usa, Hachiman was identified with Amida Buddha. Not partners, not master and disciple: one being, two faces. This is the stage in which, as [tale 8](/en/story/08-rokugo-peak/) put it, nobody could quite say whether a figure was a god or a buddha — *and nobody minded*.

Line the stages up and something delightful appears. Buddhas save the gods; then gods guard the buddhas; then gods study under the buddhas; then gods *are* the buddhas. **Four centuries of the two sides edging closer, one step at a time.** "Fusion" was never an event. It was a very long courtship.

### And nearly every milestone happened at Usa

Here is the part that makes this peninsula extraordinary. Check the milestones against the map:

- An early, splendid shrine-temple — **Miroku-ji at Usa**
- The first great public act of a god venerating a buddha — **Hachiman and the Great Buddha**, 749
- The first bodhisattva title ever granted to a kami — **Hachiman**, 781
- Buddhist rites woven into a shrine festival — Usa's **Hōjō-e**, releasing living creatures to console the war dead ([tale 3](/en/story/03-yamato/))

The laboratory of Japan's great religious fusion was not Nara, and not Kyoto. **It was Usa.**

### Why Usa? Because you could talk to this god

[Last time](/en/rabithole/03-hachiman/) gave us half the answer: Hachiman was **born mixed** — a braid of local, immigrant, and Yamato worship. A god who began as a blend had no instinct to reject the newest arrival from across the sea.

But there is a second half, easy to miss. Hachiman was a god **with a voice**. Through the oracles of Usa's priestesses, this god could *speak* — could say "I wish for the Buddhist path," could volunteer to protect the Great Buddha, could even negotiate the settlement of a stolen jewel into a temple's founding charter. Every step of that four-century courtship needed to be put into words, and at Usa, uniquely, the god's side of the conversation could be heard.

Fusion advanced fastest exactly where there was **a god you could talk with**.

### So — who finally needed the word?

Now the answer to the title question, and it comes with a date.

In 1868, the new Meiji government — building a modern nation with Shinto as its symbolic core — issued the **Separation Edict**: kami and buddhas were to be institutionally split. Shrine-temples were dismantled across the country; in the storm of anti-Buddhist iconoclasm that followed, Usa's thousand-year-old Miroku-ji was **erased** — today it is the quiet lawn of foundation stones we visited in [tale 5](/en/story/05-anotherBuddhism/).

The tidy rule you learned on day one of your trip — shrines here, temples there — **was manufactured in 1868**. And it was only after the cutting that the long marriage needed a name. *Shinbutsu-shūgō* is the word a divided age invented for what it had divided.

The good news: on Kunisaki, the un-divided world is still unusually easy to see. Guardian shrines stand inside temple precincts; Hachiman still wears his monk's robes in sculpture; and once a year the demons of a Buddhist fire rite bless the village houses. Walking this peninsula is walking the world from **before the word was necessary**.

### Today's haul from the rabbit hole

- *Shinbutsu-shūgō* is a **modern coinage** — the people who lived the fusion never needed a name for it
- The merging had **four stages**, and the relationship kept flipping: rescuers, allies, master-and-disciple, and finally one being with two faces
- Nearly every milestone happened **at Usa** — because its god was born mixed, and because he was a god **you could talk with**
- The word became necessary only after **1868**, when the fusion was forcibly cut apart

There is one more way to understand this un-divided world — not by reading, but on foot.
The monks of Rokugo Manzan left behind a walking practice that stitches the whole peninsula into a single sacred place, and it has been evolving for a thousand years.
