Rokugo Manzan did not suddenly appear. Like everything else in the story of Kunisaki, it grew gradually.

By this time, temples and mountain training sites were scattered across the peninsula. They shared many beliefs and practices, but each still functioned independently.

What transformed them into a single community?

### A Legend — and What Actually Happened

In later centuries, Rokugo Manzan would tell its own origin story. In the year 718, the story goes, a monk named **Ninmon** — an incarnation of Hachiman himself — walked the valleys of Kunisaki and founded twenty-eight temples. This is the tradition we met at the end of the last chapter: the god who took Buddhist vows, choosing these very peaks as his place of practice.

It is a beautiful story. It is also, historians agree, a story composed centuries after the fact — written down when Rokugo Manzan had grown powerful and needed a founding legend worthy of its stature. We will see why, and when, later in this tale.

The real growth was slower, quieter — and just as remarkable.

### The Network Around Miroku-ji

The story begins at **Miroku-ji**, the Buddhist temple within the Usa shrine complex. While the shrine served the kami, Miroku-ji carried out Buddhist rituals. Through it, monks and temples across the Kunisaki Peninsula maintained loose connections, sharing teachings, rituals, and pilgrimage routes.

They already formed a network. They simply did not yet think of themselves as one community.

### Mountain Buddhism Finds a Shared Language

At about the same time, Buddhism itself was changing. In the Nara period (eighth century), Buddhism had largely served the state. But during the early Heian period (ninth century), new schools of Buddhism began looking toward the mountains instead of the court.

The most influential was **Tendai Buddhism**, founded by the monk **Saichō** on Mount Hiei near Kyoto. Tendai emphasized spiritual practice in the mountains. Yet for the monks of Kunisaki, this was not an entirely new idea. They had already been climbing ridges, meditating in caves, and training among forests and cliffs for generations.

Tendai did not invent mountain practice in Kunisaki. It gave those existing traditions a shared language, a broader vision, and a place within the wider world of Japanese Buddhism.

### Six Districts, Three Roles

Gradually, the temples of the peninsula began reorganizing themselves. \
Monks traveled more frequently between valleys. \
Training methods became increasingly shared. \
Ceremonies grew more consistent.

The scattered temples slowly became a single religious community. The peninsula was organized into six districts — **Rokugō**, "the Six Districts" — and the temples took on three distinct roles:

- **Honzan** temples provided leadership and scholarship
- **Nakayama** temples led mountain training
- **Suezan** temples cared for the spiritual life of the villages

Look at the map for a moment. The training temples of Nakayama ring the central peaks, each standing where a valley meets the mountains — at the boundary between the world of villages and the world of ascetics. The pattern of the volcano, the valleys, and the faith is all there in a single image.

### A Federation, Not a Kingdom

But perhaps the most remarkable feature of Rokugo Manzan was not its structure. It was the way it governed itself.

Many religious institutions are built around a single powerful leader. Rokugo Manzan was not. Its temples worked through consultation. A head temple — the **sōzan** — served as coordinator, but important matters were settled by representatives of the temples gathering and seeking consensus.

It was less like a kingdom than a federation.

### A Living Network

More importantly, Rokugo Manzan was never simply a collection of temples.

The temples were connected by mountain trails. \
The mountains overlooked farming villages. \
The villages supported the temples. \
The temples protected the sacred mountains. \
The sacred mountains gave meaning to the landscape. \
Everything belonged to the same living network.

For the monks, the entire peninsula became a place of practice. \
For the villagers, every valley became part of a sacred world. \
The mountains were no longer simply mountains. \
The villages were no longer simply villages. \
The forests, cliffs, shrines, temples, and pilgrimage paths together formed a single religious landscape.

Perhaps this is what makes Rokugo Manzan so unusual. It was never defined by one great temple. It was defined by an entire peninsula. The world offers few parallels — Mount Athos in Greece, a peninsula given over entirely to monastic life, may be the closest cousin this place has.

The stage that volcanoes had created millions of years earlier had finally found its purpose.

Kunisaki itself had become a sacred landscape.

### Worth a Visit: The Mountain That Led Them All — Mount Saiei and Kōzan-ji

The role of sōzan — coordinator of all the Rokugo Manzan temples — is said to have first belonged to **Kōzan-ji**, on Mount Saiei, the mountain rising west of the Tashibu valley.

The mountain's name tells you everything about its ambitions: *Saiei-zan* means "Western Hiei," a deliberate echo of Mount Hiei, the great Tendai headquarters near Kyoto. Tradition even counted it among "Japan's three Hiei," alongside Mount Hiei itself and Tōeizan in Edo. In the Heian period, Kōzan-ji stood at the head of the sixty-five temples of Rokugo Manzan.

The leadership later passed to Chōan-ji, and the halls on the heights were lost to time. The present [Kōzan-ji](https://saieizan-kouzanji.com/) (site in Japanese) carries the temple's name and flame today.

Mount Saiei looks down on the Tashibu valley — the medieval estate we walked in an earlier chapter. Seen from here, the harvest in the fields below and the prayers on the mountain above were two halves of a single world.
