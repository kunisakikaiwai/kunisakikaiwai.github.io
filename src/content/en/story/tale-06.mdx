The idea did not appear overnight.

### Two Rising Powers

By this time, Buddhist temples and places of training had begun to spread around Usa and across the Kunisaki Peninsula. They did not begin as a single organization. Each temple had its own community, its own teachers, and its own traditions. But as their numbers grew, monks also began to emerge who could connect these scattered institutions and guide the people who depended on them.

And eventually, these Buddhist communities encountered another rising power.

**Usa Jingu.**

By then, Usa was no longer merely a local shrine. Through its oracles and its growing relationship with the Yamato court, Hachiman — the *kami*, or native deity, worshipped at Usa — had gained the trust of the central government. Hachiman was increasingly understood not only as the protector of Usa, but as a guardian of the state itself. The name of Hachiman began to spread far beyond Kyushu.

But Buddhism had also become one of the great pillars of the state. In Nara, monks prayed for peace, temples performed rituals for the protection of the country, and Buddhist teachings had become deeply connected with imperial authority.

The gods of Usa and the Buddhas of the capital belonged to different religious worlds. At least in theory.

### In the Villages, No Problem at All

In everyday life, ordinary people had never seen such a sharp division. They prayed to the rice-field god for the harvest and to the sea god for a safe voyage; when epidemics struck, they asked monks to chant sutras, and they entrusted their dead to the Buddha's care. Those who had crossed the sea brought their own beliefs; local people continued to honor the gods of the mountains, forests, fields, and sea.

Many did both. \
They visited shrines. \
They also visited temples. \
The thought "these are two religions — you must choose one" simply never arose.

### The State Needed an Answer

At the level of the state, however, things were different.

The court had made the worship of the kami an official institution — and had made Buddhism a pillar of national protection. And remember, from the last chapters, what the gods meant to the people of this age: theirs was a power that moved reality itself. Serve them wrongly, and curses and calamities would fall upon the state. Rulers themselves believed this in earnest. With two such powers holding up the same country, their relationship could not be left vague. If the kami and the Buddhas were rivals, how could both protect the same realm?

An answer had to be found — and for Usa, the shrine the court leaned on most, the question was urgent.

### "The Gods, Too, Protect the Dharma"

The answer that gradually emerged was elegantly simple. \
The kami were not enemies of Buddhism. \
They were protectors of the Buddhist teaching. \
They, too, could support the salvation of living beings.

Japan did not ask, **"Which one is true?"**

It asked, **"How can they work together?"**

In a sense, this doctrine merely put into official words what villagers had known all along. The lived reality of the countryside was translated into a language the state could accept. This way of thinking would become known as **shinbutsu shūgō** — the joining of kami and Buddhas.

The idea transformed Usa. A Buddhist temple, **Miroku-ji**, rose within the shrine complex itself — the temple of Maitreya promised in the last chapter. A shrine for the kami and a temple for the Buddhas, side by side: not confusion, but cooperation.

### The Day a God Bowed to the Buddha

Then came the event that displayed this new partnership to the whole nation.

If you have ever stood before the Great Buddha of Nara — or plan to — here is a story that may change how you see it.

In the mid-eighth century, Emperor Shōmu vowed to cast a colossal bronze Buddha, a project meant to place the entire nation under the Buddha's protection. It nearly failed. The court struggled to find enough copper, enough gold for the gilding, enough labor and treasure to see it through.

Then an oracle arrived from Usa. Hachiman had spoken:

*"I shall lead the gods of heaven and earth, and see the Great Buddha completed."*

A regional kami had pledged the power of Japan's gods to the greatest Buddhist project the country had ever attempted. When gold — needed for the gilding — was soon discovered in the northeast, people took it as proof that the god of Usa was keeping his word.

In 749, Hachiman traveled to the capital. The god's spirit was carried in a purple palanquin attended by Ōga no Morime, a priestess of Usa, and welcomed by the court with the honors of a head of state. And then, before the rising Great Buddha, the god of Usa **worshipped**.

A kami, bowing before the Buddha — performed in public, at the center of the nation. This was shinbutsu shūgō stepping onto the national stage. The court granted Hachiman a rank on par with the imperial family, and beside Tōdai-ji a shrine was raised where Hachiman keeps watch over the Great Buddha to this day.

### The God Who Took Buddhist Vows

The bond between Hachiman and the Buddha then went one step deeper.

A new idea was spreading: the kami themselves were beings caught in suffering, longing for the Buddha's salvation. Hachiman, tradition says, expressed through oracles the wish to take refuge in the Buddhist path.

In 781, the court bestowed on Hachiman the title **Great Bodhisattva** — a being advancing toward Buddhist enlightenment. No kami had ever received such a title before.

In time, Hachiman came to be depicted with a shaved head and monk's robes: the [monk-form Hachiman](https://www.nippon.com/ja/japan-topics/b10935/) (*sōgyō Hachiman*; the link shows the National Treasure statue at Tōdai-ji, carved by the master sculptor Kaikei — article in Japanese, but the photographs speak for themselves). A god who took Buddhist vows: a form of faith with few parallels anywhere in the world.

Not merely protecting the Dharma, but walking the Buddha's path himself — here the union of kami and Buddha reached its summit.

### A Shared World

This partnership helped transform Hachiman into one of the most widely worshipped deities in Japan; shrines bearing his name eventually spread across the entire country. In later centuries, warriors would adopt Hachiman as their protector, giving rise to his famous image as a god of war. But that came later.

What mattered now was this: the old gods had not been defeated, and the new religion had not replaced them. Both were being woven into a single shared world.

In Kunisaki, that shared world would soon take physical form. A powerful shrine stood in Usa. Temples and mountain ascetics were spreading across the peninsula. Now they needed a way to belong to one another — and that way would become **Rokugo Manzan**.

And tradition whispers one more thing. When Hachiman took his vows and sought a place to practice, the mountains he chose were none other than the peaks of Kunisaki.

### Worth a Visit: The Shrine That Guards the Great Buddha — Tamukeyama Hachiman-gū

The story of Hachiman and the Great Buddha has a sequel you can still visit today — not in Kyushu, but in Nara.

On the hillside just east of the Great Buddha Hall of Tōdai-ji stands [Tamukeyama Hachiman-gū](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamukeyama_Hachimang%C5%AB), founded in 749 when Hachiman was welcomed from Usa to protect the great temple. For more than 1,250 years, the god from the Kunisaki region's neighborhood has kept watch over Japan's most famous Buddha.

The shrine stands along the quiet walking path from the Great Buddha Hall toward Nigatsu-dō hall, and has long been loved as a spot for autumn foliage.

If your travels take you to Nara, pay the Great Buddha a visit — and then take the short walk uphill, to greet the god who once promised to see it built.
