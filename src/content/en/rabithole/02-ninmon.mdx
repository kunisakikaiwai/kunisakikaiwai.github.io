He has walked through every corner of [the Tale of Kunisaki](/en/story/): the monk who founded twenty-eight temples in a single year, the incarnation of the god Hachiman, the inventor of the fire festival, the guide who marked out the pilgrimage route — **Ninmon Bosatsu**.

And in [the seventh tale](/en/story/07-rokugoStarted/) we made a promise: his legend, we said, was "composed centuries after the fact," and we would explain later. This is later. Time to open the box — not to ask whether the legend is *true*, but to ask a better question: **who wrote it down, when, and why?**

### What form does the legend actually take?

Not fireside folklore. **Books.**

The centerpiece is a work with a magnificent title: the *Compendium of Oracles of Hachiman of Usa* (Hachiman Usagū go-takusenshū), compiled in 1313 by Jin'un, a scholar-monk in the service of the Usa shrine. Sixteen volumes gathering the god's oracles and origins — and, woven into them, the story of Ninmon, the god's own incarnation, opening temples across the valleys of Kunisaki.

But note the dates. A book written in 1313, describing events of 718. That gap — six centuries — is roughly the distance between us and Joan of Arc. Imagine reconstructing her life today from hearsay, and you have the historian's problem in a nutshell. Which is why scholars read the Compendium less as a record of what happened in the eighth century, and more as a record of **what fourteenth-century people wanted the eighth century to have been**. That, it turns out, is the more interesting document anyway.

(There is also an older-looking text, the *Ninmon Bosatsu Chōki*, which some researchers treat as a source behind the Compendium — the digging continues. In the oldest manuscripts, even the characters used to write "Ninmon" differ from today's. The legend has layers all the way down.)

### Why write it down in 1313, of all times?

Readers of [the eighth tale](/en/story/08-rokugo-peak/) already know the answer in three words: **it was armor.**

- **Armor for the land** — in an age of endless lawsuits over estates, "this mountain was opened by an incarnation of Hachiman" was the best title deed a temple could hold
- **Armor for identity** — squeezed between two great patrons, the Usa shrine complex and the Tendai headquarters on Mt. Hiei, the community needed its own founding claim: *we are the mountains Ninmon opened*
- **The mood of the age** — the Compendium was compiled a generation after the Mongol invasions, the storms remembered as *kamikaze*, when Japan was convinced its gods had saved the realm and the urge to write down their deeds had never been stronger

In other words, compiling the legend was not nostalgia. It was **live paperwork** — a six-hundred-year-old founding story doing urgent legal and political work in the present.

### So what does the legend say he did?

With that lens in place, we can enjoy the story properly. In narrative order:

**1. He appears on the sacred mountain.** Ninmon's story begins on [Mt. Omoto](/en/story/02-across-sea/), the holy peak of Usa, where he appears alongside Hachiman himself. A god's incarnation, entering the mountains in a monk's robes — the peninsula's whole future in a single image.

**2. In 718, he founds twenty-eight temples.** All across the six districts, in one grand year. Why twenty-eight? Because the Lotus Sutra — the central scripture of Tendai Buddhism — has **twenty-eight chapters**. Even the number of temples is a chapter list.

**3. A dragon king brings him a thousand lamps.** As Ninmon practiced in a cliff cave, the dragon king of the sea, moved by his devotion, offered a thousand lights. The first temple took its name from them: Sento-ji, "the temple of a thousand lights" — the great ruin we walked in [the ninth tale](/en/story/09-rokugo-collapse/).

**4. He carves 69,000 buddhas.** And here the design reveals itself: the Lotus Sutra contains roughly **sixty-nine thousand characters**. Twenty-eight temples, sixty-nine thousand buddhas — the legend lays out the entire peninsula as one gigantic copy of the sutra, valleys for chapters, buddhas for written characters. The numbers are suspiciously tidy — always the scent of a story polished after the fact — but the sheer scale of the metaphor commands respect.

**5. He founds the demon rite.** Gathering the monks of all twenty-eight temples, he hands down the "six scrolls of the demon rite" and establishes **Shujō Onie** — the fire festival where, uniquely in Japan, [the demons are welcomed rather than driven out](/en/story/08-rokugo-peak/). Why are Kunisaki's demons kind? Because, says the legend, their festival was founded by a saint.

**6. He dies where he began.** His work done, Ninmon enters final rest in the "Pillow Cave" behind Sento-ji — the very first temple he founded. Among the thousand stone pagodas in those woods, one is still pointed out as his grave. A life that closes exactly where it opened: as storytelling, flawless.

**7. He keeps appearing.** The story refuses to end. In 855, a century after his death, Ninmon appears before a monk and personally marks out the route of the peaks — the origin of the [mine-iri pilgrimage](/en/story/07-rokugoStarted/). And to this day, the demons of Shujō Onie are said to be Ninmon and his fellows in another guise. Which means he still shows up, once a year, carrying a torch.

### Today's haul from the rabbit hole

- The legend lives in **books**: above all the *Compendium of Oracles* (1313) — written six centuries after the events, as far from them as we are from Joan of Arc
- It was written because **it was armor**: a founding story doing live legal work in an age of land disputes and post-Mongol fervor
- The tale itself is a masterpiece of design: Lotus Sutra numerology, a perfect narrative circle from first temple to deathbed — and a founder who **never quite left**

Next question down the hole: the god who sent Ninmon as his incarnation — Hachiman. Before he became Japan's great war god, worshipped at half the shrines in the country… what exactly was he? That turns out to be the deepest rabbit hole on the peninsula.
