Spend a day on the Kunisaki Peninsula and you will meet the words **Rokugo Manzan** everywhere — on temple signboards, on trail markers, in every brochure. Guides usually translate it as something like "the six-district mountain temples" and move briskly on.

Let's not move on. This is the rabbit hole, after all. Taken apart, the four characters of 六郷満山 hide three good riddles: an administrative map as old as Nara's Great Buddha, a Buddhist word for "everyone on the mountain" — and a name that is far younger than it looks.

### "Rokugo" is six districts — on a 1,300-year-old map

In the eighth century, while the court at Nara was casting the Great Buddha, the young Japanese state was rolling out a Chinese-style administrative system: provinces, counties, and local districts called *gō* (郷). The Kunisaki Peninsula became Kunisaki County of Bungo Province, divided into **six districts: Kunawa, Tashibu, Imi, Kunisaki, Musashi, and Aki**. A tenth-century dictionary duly lists all six. That is "Rokugo": *the six gō*.

Look where the boundaries run. The six districts ring **Mt. Futago**, the extinct volcano at the peninsula's heart — one district per fan of radiating valleys, the terrain we met in [the first tale](/en/story/01-volcano/). The tax office, you might say, simply traced the lines the volcano had already drawn.

### Those names are still on the mail

Here is the part I like best. All six district names are **still in everyday use as addresses**. The city of Kunisaki keeps Kunisaki, Musashi, and Aki as its town names; Imi lives on in Kunimi Town; Bungotakada keeps Tashibu and Kunawa.

England still calls its towns Chester and Lancaster after Roman army camps. Kunisaki goes one better: here, virtually the whole eighth-century district map survives intact, and people write those names on their parcels every day. A 1,300-year-old address book, still in service.

### So who named the mountains after a tax map?

Now the second riddle. In the medieval registers and temple rosters — written from around the twelfth century onward — the temple network calls itself **Rokugō-san, "the Mountains of the Six Districts."** Nobody in Nara or Kyoto assigned that name. The monks chose it themselves.

And there is a lovely irony in the choice. These were estate lands that had quietly slipped out of the state's tax system — the "[Donate the Name, Keep the Land](/en/story/04-shoen/)" world of the fourth tale. Yet when those same communities needed a name, they reached for the state's own administrative label. Not defiance, not submission — they simply borrowed the official signboard and put it to work. This is speculation, to be fair. But it smells like the same canny instinct.

One more detail: when the temple precincts were recently designated a National Historic Site, the official name chosen was **"Rokugō-san"** — exactly as in the medieval sources. Scholars and officials still use the old name.

### Then where does "Manzan" come from?

*Manzan* — literally "the whole mountain" — is a Buddhist collective noun: all the temples and all the monks of a sacred mountain, taken as one body.

And here is the third riddle's answer: the four-character combination **"Rokugo Manzan" only became common in modern times**. Right through the Edo period, the documents say "Rokugō-san." The famous name on all the brochures is really a modern welding of two worlds — a bureaucrat's district map and a monk's word for the mountain community.

Which, honestly, makes it a rather perfect name. The administrative grid and the world of prayer, fused into a single word: 1,300 years of this peninsula, in four characters.

### Today's haul from the rabbit hole

- **Rokugo** = six eighth-century districts (Kunawa, Tashibu, Imi, Kunisaki, Musashi, Aki) — still valid addresses today
- In the sources, the network's name for itself is **Rokugō-san** — a self-chosen name, and still the official one on the National Historic Site register
- **"Rokugo Manzan" is a modern coinage**: a tax map plus a Buddhist word, welded together
